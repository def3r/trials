01

It doesn't just claim factors — it finds the right ones, prime or composite

c2q_factor runs a Grover search over superposed pairs (a, b), phase-flipping states where a QFT-based multiplier confirms a×b = n, then reads off the most-amplified pair. For a prime it correctly converges on the trivial pair (1, n) — there's nothing else for the oracle to amplify. Sampled across five qubit tiers (a composite and a prime or two per tier), plus n=15 repeated five times back to back to rule out run-to-run drift:

02

Cost climbs in qubit-count steps — and the steps are steep

Qubit count isn't continuous in n: c2q_factor allocates total_q = 4×num_state + 1 qubits, where num_state tracks n's bit length. Every time n crosses a power of two, the circuit jumps up a whole tier — five composites in a row inside one tier cost about the same; the next composite, one bit longer, costs roughly 30× more. By the 25-qubit tier (n in the 64–127 range, the top of what the 28-qubit simulator limit allows) a single call takes the better part of a minute.

03

The comparison that actually matters: what does it cost to skip the quantum call

Trial division up to √n is the least clever classical factoring algorithm that exists, and for n ≤ 127 it never checks more than 11 candidate divisors. Measured on the same machine: an average of ~6 nanoseconds per call across the full 4–127 range — fast enough that it's competing with the resolution of the clock measuring it, not with any real computational limit.

This is the opposite shape from maxcut, TSP, kcolor, and clique. Those four target genuinely NP-hard problems where brute force becomes impractical fast — a 20-city TSP tour has 19! orderings, a 25-vertex graph has 225 subsets to check for a clique. Classical cost there explodes well inside sizes a person would actually want to run. Factoring n ≤ 127 has no such wall: √127 ≈ 11, so the "hard" case is eleven modulus operations. The kernel isn't competing against an expensive classical algorithm here — it's competing against a for-loop that finishes before the quantum circuit is done being constructed.

04

The ceiling is real, and it's exactly where the code says it is

c2q_factor(128) throws immediately, before any simulation runs:

c2q_factor: n requires 29 qubits (simulator limit 28); safe range is n ≤ 127 (num_result ≤ 7)

That's the entire usable domain, top to bottom: 4 through 127, no exceptions, no partial credit for numbers just past it.

Directly answering what was asked: yes, it factors 15 and everything above it correctly — correctness holds across the entire testable range, all the way to the n = 127 ceiling, with zero degradation (unlike kcolor and clique, this kernel doesn't get less reliable as it gets bigger, only slower). It stays fast — sub-second — through n = 63 (21 qubits); the last tier before the wall, n = 64–127, still returns the right answer every time but costs on the order of 46 seconds per call.

A factor_pass.cpp would be recognizing structurally valid Grover targets and routing them to a call that is, at minimum, a quarter-million times slower than the trial-division loop it replaced — and up to 7.6 billion times slower at the top of its own range. That's not a reliability problem the way it was for kcolor and clique; it's a value proposition problem. The kernel's actual asymptotic edge only shows up at cryptographically relevant bit-lengths, hundreds of qubits away from where a state-vector simulator can ever take it. Inside the only range this kernel can run, brute-force classical factoring wins by five to ten orders of magnitude, every time. Recommend not building the pass.
